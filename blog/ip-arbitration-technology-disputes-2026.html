IP Arbitration for Technology Disputes:
Arbitrability, AI Assets & Drafting Traps

Selin Özgören Özgören Law Firm April 2026 8 min read
← Back to Knowledge Bank
Key Takeaways
01
Arbitrability is binary

Under HMK Art. 408 and SMK, licence disputes and damages claims are arbitrable. Patent invalidity is not — but a tribunal can address it as a preliminary matter with inter partes effect, which resolves most commercial IP disputes.

02
AI ownership is unresolved

In 2026, neither training data ownership nor the copyright status of AI-generated outputs has been settled at the international treaty level. These disputes are landing in arbitration precisely because national courts lack specialist neutrals and cannot seal proceedings.

03
Clause scope determines outcome

The three most common IP arbitration failures — narrow clause scope, no emergency arbitrator provision, and vague evidence rules — are all drafting problems, not legal ones. They are entirely preventable.

Intellectual property rights are territorial by design. Their commercial ecosystems are not. A software licence dispute between a Turkish developer and a German distributor can simultaneously implicate the law of five jurisdictions — and produce five inconsistent court orders. IP arbitration does not eliminate that territorial complexity, but it provides the one mechanism capable of resolving it in a single proceeding, before a neutral with the technical background to understand what is actually in dispute.

What Is — and Is Not — Arbitrable Under Turkish Law

The threshold question in any IP arbitration is whether the dispute can be submitted to arbitration at all. Under Turkish law, the answer turns on two provisions read together: Article 408 of the Code of Civil Procedure (HMK), which defines the general scope of arbitrability by reference to the parties' freedom to dispose of their rights, and the Industrial Property Law (SMK, Law No. 6769), which governs patents, trademarks, designs, and geographical indications.

The arbitrability line in practice

The distinction Turkish law draws is between rights the parties may freely settle — and rights they cannot, because those rights affect third parties and the public registral order.

Arbitrable
Not arbitrable (exclusive court jurisdiction)
Licence fee disputes Unpaid royalties, calculation disputes, most-favoured-licensee claims, audit rights under licence agreements.
Patent invalidation (hükümsüzlük) Cancellation of a patent from the TÜRKPATENT register has erga omnes effect and falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of specialised IP courts.
Contractual IP breach Breach of exclusivity, unauthorised sublicensing, failure to commercialise, post-termination IP use obligations.
Trademark invalidation Removal of a trademark registration affects the public register and cannot be ordered by arbitral award with third-party effect.
Damages for infringement Monetary compensation for infringement between contracting parties — including lost profits, reasonable royalty, and disgorgement of the infringer's profits.
Compulsory licensing (SMK Art. 129 ff.) Applications for compulsory licence where the patentee has refused to licence on reasonable terms — a public interest determination reserved for courts.
Trade secret misappropriation Claims under the Turkish Commercial Code and SMK Art. 82 ff. for the unlawful disclosure, acquisition, or use of confidential technical information.
Ownership disputes affecting the register Where resolution requires alteration of the registered owner on the TÜRKPATENT record — distinct from a contractual ownership dispute between the parties.
A tribunal may assess patent validity as a preliminary issue when it is raised as a defence to a licence fee claim. Its determination will bind the parties to that arbitration — inter partes — but cannot be used to cancel the patent from the register. For resolving commercial IP disputes between contracting parties, this is ordinarily sufficient.

Why Technology Companies Choose Arbitration Over Litigation

The preference for arbitration in technology IP disputes is not a default to familiarity. It reflects specific structural features of this category of dispute that court litigation handles poorly.

Specialist neutrals

A general civil court judge brings expertise in civil procedure. An IP arbitration tribunal can include a patent attorney with a background in semiconductor physics, a former software engineer turned lawyer, and a specialist in licensing economics. For disputes that turn on whether a rival product actually uses the patented method — or whether a training dataset genuinely contains the claimant's protected expression — tribunal composition is outcome-relevant in a way it rarely is in ordinary commercial disputes.

Confidentiality as structural protection

In court litigation, the disclosure process is inherently public. A party asserting trade secret misappropriation must, to some extent, reveal the secret it claims was taken. Hearings examining source code or proprietary algorithms occur in open courtrooms. IP arbitration proceedings, by contrast, are private by default under all major institutional rules, and confidentiality obligations can be extended by agreement to cover all disclosed materials, including technical exhibits and expert reports. For companies whose core commercial value is embedded in information, this is not a secondary consideration.

The question is not whether arbitration is preferable to litigation in the abstract. The question is whether your dispute — with its specific technical subject matter, cross-border enforcement needs, and confidentiality constraints — is better resolved by a generalist judge applying procedural rules designed for contract disputes, or by a specialist tribunal operating under rules designed for exactly this.

The New York Convention enforcement advantage

A judgment from a Turkish court faces recognition and enforcement obstacles in jurisdictions with which Turkey has no bilateral treaty, or where reciprocity has not been established in practice. An arbitral award carries none of those obstacles: under the New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards (to which 172 states are party as of 2026), the award is enforceable in any contracting state on a largely standardised basis. For technology companies whose infringing counterpart operates assets across multiple jurisdictions, this matters considerably.

The 2026 Frontier: AI Dataset Ownership and Generated-Content Disputes

In 2025–2026, a new category of IP dispute emerged at volume that had previously been theoretical: disputes over the ownership of AI training datasets, the copyright status of AI-generated outputs, and the contractual allocation of IP rights in AI development agreements. Courts across Europe, the United States, and Asia are still producing first-instance decisions in this space. International treaty frameworks have not been updated. The legal landscape is genuinely unsettled.

Three unresolved questions driving arbitration demand

  • Training data and the copyright status of inputs Using third-party data to train an AI model may or may not constitute copyright infringement depending on which jurisdiction's law applies. EU law provides a text-and-data mining exception under the 2019 Copyright Directive (Art. 4), subject to rights-holder opt-out. US law applies a fair use analysis that remains unsettled after several significant district court rulings in 2024–2025. Turkish copyright law (FSEK) provides no equivalent exception, leaving the position uncertain. A cross-border training data dispute will involve all three regimes simultaneously — and arbitration provides the only forum capable of addressing them in a single proceeding.
  • Copyright in AI-generated outputs Under the Berne Convention framework and the domestic law of most jurisdictions — including Turkey — copyright requires a human author. AI-generated content produced without meaningful human creative input is not protected. Where human contribution is present but partial (prompt engineering, curation, editorial selection), the scope of protection — and its ownership — is contested. Companies building on AI-generated assets need contractual clarity that no registry can currently provide.
  • IP ownership in AI development agreements Who owns the model weights produced by a joint AI development project? Who owns improvements to a licensed model made by the licensee's development team? Does a data-sharing agreement transfer the IP embedded in the contributor's data? These questions are now appearing in commercial disputes with frequency, and they require bespoke contractual analysis — not default statutory rules, which in most jurisdictions were not designed with machine-learning outputs in mind.
The WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center updated its Expedited Arbitration Rules in 2023, reducing the standard timeline to six to nine months from tribunal constitution to award for disputes submitted under those rules. The procedure is available by agreement and can be adopted after a dispute has arisen. For technology companies facing time-sensitive IP disputes — a product launch injunction, a licence termination, a dataset ownership claim — the expedited procedure materially reduces the commercial cost of arbitration.

Three Drafting Errors That Undermine IP Arbitration Clauses

The most avoidable failures in IP arbitration proceedings arise not from the law, not from the facts, and not from the tribunal. They arise from the arbitration clause itself — negotiated at the time of contracting, when the dispute was hypothetical and neither party's lawyers gave it sufficient attention.

I
Clause scope limited to "contractual disputes"

A clause that submits "disputes arising from this agreement" to arbitration will be interpreted narrowly in most jurisdictions. Tort-based infringement claims (a party infringes the other's IP outside the scope of the agreement), unjust enrichment claims arising from unlicensed use, and post-termination IP obligations may fall outside the clause's scope — leaving them for national courts to determine in parallel with the arbitration.

Draft the clause to cover "all disputes arising out of or in connection with this agreement, including disputes relating to its validity, breach, termination, or any related intellectual property rights."

II
No emergency arbitrator provision — and no carve-out for court interim relief

In IP disputes, continued infringement during the proceedings can cause irreversible commercial harm: a competing product enters the market, a trade secret is further disclosed, a parallel import network becomes established. Without either an emergency arbitrator provision or a clause preserving the right to seek court interim relief, the claimant may be left without any protective remedy during the twelve to eighteen months it takes to constitute a full tribunal and obtain a substantive order.

Expressly adopt the emergency arbitrator procedure of the chosen institution, and separately preserve court interim relief rights under MTK Article 6 / applicable national law — these rights co-exist with the arbitration agreement and do not constitute a waiver.

III
No agreed standard for document production

Technology IP disputes frequently require disclosure of source code, training datasets, development logs, or proprietary technical documentation. Without an agreed evidence standard in the clause or the procedural timetable, parties face satellite disputes about the scope of production, the format of disclosure, and the protective order regime for sensitive materials. These disputes add cost, delay, and uncertainty — and in the worst cases, prevent access to the central evidence in the proceeding.

Specify by reference in the clause (or at the first procedural conference) that the IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence in International Arbitration will govern document production, and address source code review and confidentiality tiers from the outset.

Model clause elements for IP transactions

The following illustrates the structural elements that a well-drafted IP arbitration clause should address. This is not a template and should not be used as such — appropriate drafting depends on the specific transaction, governing law, seat, and institutional rules.

"Any dispute, controversy, or claim arising out of or relating to this Agreement, or the breach, termination, or validity thereof, including any dispute relating to intellectual property rights owned by or licensed to the parties, shall be finally resolved by arbitration under the [Institution] Rules. The seat of arbitration shall be [Seat]. The language of the arbitration shall be [Language]. The parties expressly agree that the emergency arbitrator provisions of the [Institution] Rules shall apply. Nothing in this clause shall prevent either party from seeking interim or conservatory measures from a court of competent jurisdiction before the tribunal is constituted."

Illustrative only. Not legal advice. Appropriate clause language must be tailored to the specific transaction and governing law.

The Practitioner's View

IP arbitration has matured considerably since the early institutional rules were drafted for straightforward patent licence disputes. The current caseload — AI asset ownership, cross-border software infringement, multi-party technology transfer breakdowns — reflects the genuine complexity of how technology value is created and deployed in 2026.

The legal framework, both in Turkey and internationally, is adequate for resolving these disputes through arbitration when the dispute resolution infrastructure has been properly designed. The cases that fail — those that take four years, cost disproportionately, or produce awards that cannot be enforced where the assets are — almost invariably fail because of avoidable drafting deficiencies in the arbitration clause, not because arbitration was the wrong choice.

The implication is straightforward: the investment in well-structured dispute resolution provisions at the contracting stage — including clause scope, seat selection, institutional rules, emergency procedures, and evidence standards — is material. It is also the part of the transaction that receives the least attention.
