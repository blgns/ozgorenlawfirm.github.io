How to Recognise a Foreign Divorce
in Turkey

Key Takeaways
01
Not automatic

A foreign divorce has no legal effect in Turkey until a Turkish court or civil registry formally acts. You may still be listed as married in Turkish records.

02
Two routes available

Since 2017, an administrative route exists alongside the judicial route — but it only applies to simple dissolutions where both parties agree and no financial orders need executing.

03
Five statutory conditions

You walked out of a courtroom in London, Berlin, or New York with a divorce decree in hand. In your home country, you are legally single. In Turkey, the state may still consider you married — with all the legal consequences that entails. Recognition and enforcement of a foreign divorce judgment in Turkey is not automatic. Until a Turkish court or authority formally acts, your civil registry status remains unchanged, affecting your right to remarry, property rights, inheritance position, and in some cases tax status.

What Legal Framework Governs Recognition of Foreign Divorces in Turkey?

Two primary sources of law govern recognition and enforcement of foreign divorce judgments in Turkey.

MÖHUK — Law No. 5718

Turkey's Act on Private International and Procedural Law (MÖHUK), specifically Articles 50–59, is the foundational statute. It governs the recognition and enforcement of all foreign civil judgments — including divorce decrees — through a formal court proceeding known as a tanıma davası (recognition lawsuit). The Turkish court does not retry the case: it acts as a gatekeeper, verifying whether a specific set of statutory conditions has been met.

Article 27/A — Population Services Act (NHK)

Since 2017, a faster administrative route has been available under Article 27/A of Law No. 5490. Turkish citizens (and in some cases Blue Card holders) may apply directly to the civil registry authority (NVI) to register a foreign divorce without filing a court case — provided both parties agree and the decree contains no orders requiring enforcement.

The administrative route introduced in 2017 is faster and avoids court proceedings — but it is only available for straightforward dissolutions where both parties consent. The moment your decree contains maintenance orders, property division, or custody provisions that need to operate in Turkey, you are back in court.

How Does the Recognition Process Work — Step by Step?

For the judicial route, recognition requires filing a formal lawsuit before the Family Court (Aile Mahkemesi) at the respondent's Turkish domicile. If the respondent has no Turkish address, proceedings may be filed before the courts in İzmir, Istanbul, or Ankara. In most cases the process can be conducted entirely via power of attorney — no travel to Turkey is required.

01
Identify the Correct Route

Determine whether the administrative route (Art. 27/A NHK) or judicial route (MÖHUK) applies. Key factors: consent of both parties, whether any financial provisions need enforcement, Blue Card status.

02
Prepare the Required Documents

The original apostilled decree, a certificate of finality (also apostilled), and notarised Turkish translations of both. Where ancillary orders exist, all must be separately apostilled and translated.

03
Grant Power of Attorney

If you will not attend in person, a power of attorney must be notarised and apostilled in your country of residence and granted to Turkish-admitted counsel.

04
File the Recognition Petition

The petition sets out the legal basis, confirms that the five MÖHUK conditions are satisfied, and requests the Family Court to issue a recognition order. The respondent is served and may raise objections.

05
Court Review — No Merits Examination

The Turkish court does not re-examine the grounds for divorce or the tribunal's reasoning. Review is strictly limited to whether the five statutory conditions are met.

06
Civil Registry Update

Once the recognition order becomes final, it is registered with the Turkish civil registry (Nüfus). Your marital status is updated, enabling remarriage, inheritance, and identity document processes.

What Are the Five Statutory Conditions Turkish Courts Check?

Under MÖHUK Article 54, Turkish courts examine five conditions before granting recognition. The burden of proof on most grounds rests with the party resisting recognition — but deficiencies in your own documents can also be fatal.

Conditions the court examines

Condition
What it means in practice
A — Finality of the judgmentThe decree must be final and no longer subject to ordinary appeal. A separate certificate of finality is usually required — check the format required by the issuing court. The Apostille must come from the country where the judgment was issued, not a Turkish consulate.
B — ReciprocityA reciprocity relationship must exist between Turkey and the issuing country. Reciprocity is present with most EU states and the UK but must be verified for other jurisdictions before filing.
C — No exclusive Turkish jurisdictionThe subject matter must not fall within the exclusive jurisdiction of Turkish courts. Divorce itself is generally unaffected, but ancillary orders over Turkish real property frequently trigger this condition.
D — Right to be heardThe respondent must have been properly served and given a genuine opportunity to participate in the foreign proceedings. Default judgments obtained without proper notification are vulnerable here.
E — No violation of Turkish public policy (ordre public)The judgment must not be manifestly contrary to Turkish public order. This is a narrow filter in practice — Turkish courts have recognised divorces from a wide range of legal systems. Decrees based solely on religious law without civil court transformation, and judgments reflecting principles fundamentally at odds with Turkish constitutional values, will not pass this test. Multi-jurisdictional religious divorces (get, talaq) require preliminary analysis.
Recognition is not a retrial. The Turkish judge will not re-examine the grounds for divorce or question the foreign court's reasoning — their role is solely to verify that the five statutory conditions have been satisfied.

Practical Considerations

The formal legal framework is only part of the picture. The following considerations are relevant for parties navigating recognition proceedings in Turkey.

  • Translation quality is not a formality Turkish courts have rejected recognition applications due to translation deficiencies. Documents must be translated by a sworn translator certified by the Turkish Ministry of Justice. A translation notarised abroad is not equivalent — this is the single most common and avoidable cause of rejection.
  • The Apostille must come from the right authority The Apostille on the decree and the certificate of finality must be issued by the competent authority in the country where the judgment was rendered. Many applicants mistakenly believe that a Turkish consulate can authenticate foreign court documents. It cannot.
  • Recognition vs. enforcement — know which you need Recognition (tanıma) updates your civil registry status. Enforcement (tenfiz) makes operative orders — maintenance, property transfers, compensation — executable by Turkish courts and bailiffs. If your decree contains both, the applications can often be filed together but courts treat them as legally distinct.
  • Turkish property requires separate proceedings Turkish law applies the lex situs rule — the law of the jurisdiction where immovable property is located governs its division. A foreign court's ruling on a Turkish apartment is not automatically enforceable in Turkey and requires separate proceedings applying Turkish matrimonial property law.
  • Religious divorces need careful preliminary analysis A get, talaq, or similar religious divorce that has not been transformed into a civil court judgment in the country of origin is not directly recognisable in Turkey. A separate procedural strategy is required.
Blue Card (Mavi Kart) holders — former Turkish citizens who have renounced citizenship — can in principle access the administrative route under Art. 27/A NHK, provided both parties consent and no enforcement of financial orders is required. However, their status may affect the competent authority and procedural requirements. Legal advice is recommended before choosing between routes.

The Bottom Line

Turkey's recognition framework is well-defined and, in straightforward cases, entirely manageable. But the process demands procedural precision — and the most common failures are avoidable ones: a translation from the wrong type of translator, a certificate of finality in the wrong format, an apostille from the wrong authority.

The most effective approach is to audit the document bundle before filing, rather than discover deficiencies after a rejection. If your situation involves Turkish property, religious divorce instruments, or a Blue Card, preliminary legal analysis will determine which route applies and what additional steps are required.
