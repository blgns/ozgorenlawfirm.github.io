The Role of a Tribunal Secretary in Ensuring Enforceable Awards

Selin Özgören Özgören Law Firm April 2026 6 min read
← Back to Knowledge Bank
Key Takeaways
01
Procedural record, not just efficiency

The secretary's most consequential contribution is not speed — it is the completeness and consistency of the procedural record. That record is the primary evidence base for resisting set-aside and non-enforcement applications under the New York Convention.

02
The delegation boundary is outcome-determinative

The allegation of improper delegation does not require direct evidence of wrongdoing. It can be constructed from circumstantial indicators. The discipline with which the tribunal manages its working relationship with the secretary is the defence against this challenge — and it must be built into the appointment from the start.

03
Virtual hearings require a digital log

Remote and hybrid hearing formats have introduced a category of procedural risk that most institutional rules did not anticipate. A contemporaneous log of technical conditions, participation, and interruptions is no longer optional — it is the evidence that answers a "denial of opportunity to be heard" challenge before it is filed.

Appointing a tribunal secretary is, for many arbitrators, a practical decision: complex cases generate large volumes of correspondence, procedural orders, and documents, and managing that volume alongside the substantive work of deciding the dispute is genuinely difficult. That rationale is sound. But it understates what a well-functioning secretary actually contributes. In jurisdictions where set-aside and enforcement challenges are increasingly common, the procedural record a secretary builds throughout a case has become one of the most important factors in whether an award survives judicial scrutiny.

The Scope of the Role

There is no single universal definition of what a tribunal secretary does, and the scope varies between institutions, between cases, and between individual tribunals. What is consistent across contexts is the distinction between administrative functions — which a secretary may appropriately perform — and judicial functions — which the secretary may not.

Administrative functions

Administrative functions include maintaining the procedural timetable and monitoring compliance; managing the correspondence record and ensuring all communications are properly routed and archived; coordinating the document production phase, typically by preparing and circulating the Redfern Schedule under the IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence; liaising with the administering institution on procedural and logistical matters; preparing summaries of party submissions for the tribunal's use; and conducting legal research at the tribunal's direction.

Judicial functions — the absolute limit

Evaluating the weight of evidence, assessing the credibility of witnesses, determining which arguments are persuasive, and drafting the reasoning of awards — these belong to the arbitrators alone. This line is the central legal constraint governing the entire appointment. It is also the line most frequently tested when a losing party decides to challenge an award.

The distinction between administrative and judicial functions is not always self-evident in practice. In long, document-heavy cases, the secretary will inevitably develop a detailed understanding of the record, and tribunals reasonably rely on that knowledge. The question is not whether the secretary is involved, but whether the arbitrators remain the genuine authors of every decision. A well-functioning secretary supports the tribunal's deliberation without substituting for it.

The Delegation Boundary: Why It Determines the Award's Vulnerability

The allegation of improper delegation is among the most serious challenges a losing party can bring against an award. The argument — that the secretary, rather than the tribunal, was the effective decision-maker — does not require direct evidence of wrongdoing. It can be constructed from circumstantial indicators: a secretary who appears to have done more than administrative work, a tribunal that does not appear to have engaged closely with the record, or a pattern of procedural orders that look like they were drafted without meaningful arbitrator input.

The institutional framework

The ICC addressed this directly in its Note on the Appointment, Duties and Remuneration of Administrative Secretaries, which states explicitly that the secretary's tasks must not include work that forms part of the tribunal's judicial function. The Yukos arbitration (PCA Cases AA 226–228) brought this concern into sharp relief at the international level: Russia's application to set aside the awards included grounds of alleged improper delegation to a tribunal assistant, making clear that this is not a theoretical risk. Similar guidance appears in the LCIA Rules (Article 14A), the ICDR Guidelines (2022), and the Young ICCA Guide on Arbitral Secretaries, all of which share the same core principle: the arbitrators must be the genuine authors of every decision, and the procedural record should make this demonstrable.

The question a set-aside court asks is not whether the secretary was involved. It is whether the arbitrators were the genuine authors of the decision — and whether the record proves it.

Building the discipline into the working relationship

In practice, this means arbitrators should exercise care about how they work with a secretary. Instructions given to a secretary, documents transmitted for preparation, and the tribunal's subsequent treatment of whatever the secretary produces should all be capable of reconstruction if a challenge arises. This is not onerous — it is simply good procedural hygiene. But it is worth building into the working relationship from the outset, rather than trying to reconstruct it after an objection has been raised.

A secretary who understands this boundary and applies it consistently is an asset precisely because the protection runs in both directions: the tribunal can rely on the secretary's work without worrying that it creates exposure, and the secretary's documentation of the process provides the evidence base for rebutting a delegation challenge if one is ever brought.

Building the Enforcement Record

An arbitral award rendered in one country is frequently sought to be enforced in another. Under the New York Convention, the grounds on which a court may refuse recognition or enforcement are limited — but two of them are directly addressed by the procedural record: the argument that a party was not given proper notice of the proceedings, and the argument that a party was unable to present its case.

Both grounds turn on documentation. A complete, timestamped correspondence record demonstrating that every procedural communication was properly sent and received; a file showing that each party was notified of hearings, deadlines, and procedural decisions in accordance with the applicable rules; evidence that extensions and procedural accommodations were granted consistently and without discrimination — this is the material that answers those objections before they are even raised.

Enforcement in Turkey — additional formal requirements

For awards that may eventually be enforced in Turkey, additional considerations apply. Under MÖHUK Article 62, Turkish courts reviewing a foreign award will examine whether the proceedings were conducted consistently with Turkish public policy and whether the formal conditions for enforcement are met. Courts have declined enforcement in cases involving defects in the form of the award, ambiguities in the designation of the seat, or questions about the authority of the signatories. A pre-award formal review — checking the award against the requirements of the anticipated enforcement jurisdiction before signatures are affixed — is a straightforward step that eliminates an easily avoidable category of risk.

The secretary's maintenance of the procedural file is not administrative paperwork. It is the evidential foundation for resisting a set-aside or non-enforcement application. Arbitrators who treat file management as a ministerial function underestimate how much work it does when the award is tested.

Document Production and the Redfern Schedule

Document production is the phase of international arbitration proceedings most likely to generate subsequent complaints about procedural fairness, and it is the phase where secretarial involvement tends to be most intensive.

Managing the Redfern Schedule requires tracking requests, responses, and objections across multiple parties and rounds, ensuring that each party's positions are accurately recorded, and preparing the schedule in a form that allows the tribunal to rule efficiently and consistently. Where this is done carefully, the document production phase produces a clear record of what was requested, what was produced, what was withheld and on what grounds, and how the tribunal resolved each disputed item.

That record matters at the enforcement stage. A party that later claims it was denied access to documents it needed, or that the production process was conducted unfairly, will find it significantly harder to sustain that argument when the record shows precisely how each request was handled and that the tribunal's rulings were applied consistently across all parties.

Virtual and Hybrid Hearings

Remote and hybrid hearing formats are now standard in international arbitration, and they have introduced a category of procedural risk that existing rules did not fully anticipate. The core due process question — was every party given a genuine and equal opportunity to participate — is unchanged. The evidence needed to answer it is different.

What the digital log must capture

A contemporaneous log of hearing logistics should record who was present, in what format, at each stage of the proceedings; any technical interruptions that occurred and how they were addressed; whether all parties confirmed their ability to see and hear the proceedings before substantive content began; and how exhibits and documents were displayed and confirmed as received.

This documentation does not resolve every possible challenge, but it provides the factual foundation for the tribunal to demonstrate, if required, that the hearing was conducted fairly and that every party had a genuine opportunity to present its case. The IBA Guidelines on Videoconference in International Arbitration (2021) offer a useful procedural framework, and secretaries working on cases with remote elements should be familiar with them.

Four Points to Verify Before Confirming an Appointment

The appointment decision is where the tribunal's control is greatest. What follows are the considerations that have the most practical impact on the working relationship and the durability of the award.

01
Relevant experience with the procedural framework

Experience with the level of procedural complexity the case involves and with the applicable institutional rules matters more than subject-matter familiarity. A secretary who has never worked under ICC or HKIAC rules will require time the timetable may not accommodate. Familiarity with the likely enforcement jurisdictions — and their formal requirements — is an additional consideration for cross-border cases.

Does the proposed secretary have direct experience with this institution's procedural rules and the anticipated enforcement jurisdiction?

02
Understanding of the delegation boundary

The tribunal should satisfy itself that the secretary understands where the boundary between administrative and judicial functions lies — not in the abstract, but in the specific context of the tasks they will be asked to perform. A secretary who has read the ICC Notes and the Young ICCA Guide and can articulate the distinction in practice is markedly less likely to create exposure than one who has not.

Can the secretary articulate a concrete example of a task they would decline on delegation grounds?

03
A shared understanding of the working structure

Before confirming the appointment, there should be clarity on how instructions will be issued, how the secretary's work product will be reviewed and genuinely adopted (not merely countersigned) by the tribunal, and how that review process will be documented. These are not bureaucratic formalities — they are the record that answers a delegation challenge.

Is there a shared understanding of how the tribunal's review of the secretary's work will be recorded and documented?

04
Transparency with the parties from the outset

Most institutional frameworks now either require or strongly recommend that parties be informed of the proposed appointment, given the secretary's CV and proposed role description, and afforded an opportunity to object before the appointment proceeds. Where rules are silent, disclosure remains good practice. Transparency at the outset reduces the risk of objections later and substantially weakens any subsequent delegation challenge built on the claim that the parties were not informed.

Have the parties been given the opportunity to review and consent to the appointment as required by the applicable institutional rules?

Frequently Asked Questions

Permissible tasks fall on a spectrum. At one end, purely administrative duties — timetabling, correspondence, hearing logistics, maintaining case files — are universally accepted across all major institutions. The Young ICCA Guide on Arbitral Secretaries (2014) recognises that secretaries may also conduct legal research, review submissions, summarise evidence, attend tribunal deliberations, and assist with drafting procedural orders, provided the tribunal expressly authorises these tasks and discloses them to the parties.

The absolute limit across all institutions: the secretary must never exercise the tribunal's decision-making function or substitute for the arbitrators' personal judgment on any matter they are called upon to decide.
The "fourth arbitrator" concern arises when a secretary becomes so involved in a case that they effectively exercise influence over the tribunal's decision-making without the parties' knowledge or consent. This was at issue in the Yukos arbitration (PCA Cases AA 226–228), where Russia sought to set aside the awards partly on grounds of alleged improper delegation to a tribunal assistant.

The risk is managed through two mechanisms: transparency — parties must be informed of the secretary's role from the outset — and the tribunal's disciplined approach to reviewing and genuinely adopting, rather than simply countersigning, whatever the secretary produces. The procedural record of this review process is the primary defence against a delegation challenge.
Under the New York Convention, the grounds on which a court may refuse recognition or enforcement include the argument that a party was not given proper notice of the proceedings, and the argument that a party was unable to present its case. Both grounds turn on documentation.

A complete, timestamped correspondence record demonstrating that every procedural communication was properly sent and received, and a file showing that each party was notified of hearings and deadlines in accordance with the applicable rules, directly answers these objections. The secretary's maintenance of this record is therefore not administrative paperwork — it is the evidential foundation for resisting a set-aside or non-enforcement application.
Before confirming an appointment, arbitrators should satisfy themselves that: the secretary has relevant experience with the level of procedural complexity involved and with the applicable institutional rules; the secretary understands the delegation boundary and has a working approach to staying within it; there is a shared understanding of how instructions will be given and how the secretary's work product will be reviewed and adopted by the tribunal; and the parties have been informed of the appointment and given an opportunity to object, as required or recommended by the applicable institutional rules.

The Practitioner's View

A tribunal secretary's contribution to a case is most visible in the efficiency of the proceedings and most important in the durability of the award. The two are related: proceedings that are well-administered produce a record that is complete, consistent, and capable of withstanding scrutiny. That record is not a bureaucratic by-product of the arbitration — it is part of what makes the award enforceable.

Arbitrators who treat the secretary appointment with the same care they bring to other procedural decisions will find that the working relationship strengthens both the conduct of the proceedings and the resilience of the outcome. The questions that matter — does this secretary understand the delegation boundary, how will we document our review of their work, have the parties been properly informed — are not difficult questions. They are simply ones that need to be answered at the beginning of the case, not after a challenge has been filed.
